[e001] core
In which layer of the atmosphere is the protective ozone layer found?
- Troposphere
* Stratosphere
- Mesosphere
- Thermosphere
> The ozone layer sits in the stratosphere, roughly 6 to 30 miles above the Earth. Ozone near the ground (troposphere) is smog, not the protective layer.

[e002] core
What does the stratospheric ozone layer mainly protect people and crops from?
- Infrared heat radiated back from the Earth's surface
- Acid rain formed from sulfur and nitrogen oxides
* Ultraviolet (UV-B) radiation from the sun
- Carbon dioxide build-up
> Stratospheric ozone absorbs most of the sun's UV-B radiation. Trapping infrared heat is the greenhouse effect, a different problem.

[e003] core
Which health effect is linked to increased UV-B radiation reaching the ground because of ozone depletion?
- Higher blood pressure
* More skin cancer and cataracts
- Hearing loss
- Kidney stones
> Extra UV-B causes more skin cancers and cataracts and can weaken the immune system. It also harms crops and marine life.

[e004] core
Which element released from CFC and HCFC molecules destroys stratospheric ozone?
- Fluorine
- Hydrogen
- Carbon
* Chlorine
> UV light breaks CFC and HCFC molecules apart in the stratosphere and frees chlorine atoms. Each chlorine atom can destroy many thousands of ozone molecules in a chain reaction.

[e005] core
About how many ozone molecules can a single chlorine atom destroy before it is taken out of the reaction?
- About 10
- About 1,000
* About 100,000
- About 1 billion
> A chlorine atom acts as a catalyst and is not used up, so one atom can destroy about 100,000 ozone molecules.

[e006] core
Why do HCFCs have a lower ozone depletion potential (ODP) than CFCs?
* The hydrogen in HCFCs makes them less stable, so most break down in the lower atmosphere
- HCFCs contain no chlorine at all
- HCFCs are heavier than air and never rise above ground level
- HCFCs contain bromine, which cancels out the effect of the chlorine
> Hydrogen makes HCFC molecules less stable, so most of them break down before reaching the stratosphere. They still contain chlorine, which is why their ODP is low but not zero.

[e007] core
Natural sources such as oceans and volcanoes release large amounts of chlorine. Why is stratospheric chlorine still blamed mainly on CFCs?
- Natural chlorine is heavier and stays near the poles, where there is no ozone
* Natural chlorine compounds mostly dissolve in water and rain out before reaching the stratosphere
- Natural chlorine turns directly into ozone when it is exposed to sunlight
- Volcanoes release fluorine rather than chlorine
> Chlorine from natural sources is mostly water-soluble and is washed out of the lower atmosphere. CFCs are insoluble and stable, so they carry their chlorine up to the stratosphere, where CFC fragments have been measured.

[e008] core
Which refrigerant has an ozone depletion potential (ODP) of zero?
- R-12
- R-22
- R-11
* R-134a
> R-134a is an HFC: it contains no chlorine, so its ODP is zero. R-12 and R-11 are CFCs and R-22 is an HCFC.

[e009] core
Global warming potential (GWP) values compare a gas's warming effect over 100 years with the same mass of:
- Methane, which is given a GWP of 100
* Carbon dioxide, which is given a GWP of 1
- CFC-11, which is given a GWP of 1
- Water vapor, which is given a GWP of 1
> GWP uses carbon dioxide (GWP 1) as its reference. CFC-11 is the reference for ozone depletion potential (ODP 1.0), not for GWP.

[e010] core

* They are potent greenhouse gases with high global warming potentials
- They are flammable in air at room temperature
> HFCs have an ODP of zero but a high GWP (R-410A is about 2,088 and R-404A about 3,922). That is why the AIM Act phases them down.

[e026] core
Refrigerant blends whose ASHRAE number is in the 400 series (such as R-404A, R-407C and R-454B) are:
- Azeotropes that boil at one temperature
- Pure, single-component refrigerants
* Zeotropes, whose components boil at different temperatures
- Hydrocarbon mixtures only
> 400-series blends are zeotropic: their components have different boiling points, so the blend shows temperature glide and can fractionate. 500-series blends are azeotropes.

[e027] core
"Temperature glide" in a zeotropic refrigerant blend means:
- The refrigerant's temperature rises steadily as it sits in the cylinder
* The blend evaporates or condenses over a range of temperatures at a constant pressure
- The compressor discharge temperature drifts upward over time
- Superheat and subcooling are always equal
> Because the components boil at different temperatures, a zeotrope changes phase over a temperature range (glide) at one pressure instead of at a single temperature.

[e028] core
"Fractionation" of a refrigerant blend refers to:
- Blending two refrigerants in the field to make a new one
- Liquid refrigerant flooding back to the compressor
- Oil separating from refrigerant in the evaporator
* One component leaking or boiling off faster than the others, changing the blend's composition
> When a zeotrope leaks or is removed as vapor, the components escape at different rates and the remaining mixture changes. That is why zeotropes are charged as liquid.

[e029] core
How should a zeotropic blend such as R-407C or R-454B normally be removed from its cylinder for charging?
- As vapor from the top of the cylinder, so liquid cannot reach the compressor
* As liquid, so the blend leaves the cylinder in its correct composition
- As vapor, after warming the cylinder with a torch
- It does not matter, because zeotropes do not fractionate
> Liquid charging keeps the blend's proportions. Liquid fed into the suction side must be metered in slowly so it vaporizes before reaching the compressor. Never heat a cylinder with a torch.

[e030] core
An azeotropic refrigerant blend, such as R-502 or R-500, behaves:
* Like a single substance, boiling at one temperature at a given pressure
- Like a zeotrope, with several degrees of glide
- Like a hydrocarbon that must always be vented
- Like water in the system
> Azeotropes (500 series) act like one compound and do not noticeably fractionate. ASHRAE numbers zeotropic blends in the 400 series.

[e031] core
R-454B, one of the main replacements for R-410A in new residential systems, is a blend of:
- R-32 and R-125
* R-32 and R-1234yf
- R-134a and R-1234ze
- R-290 and R-600a
> R-454B is about 69% R-32 and 31% R-1234yf. It is a mildly flammable (A2L) zeotrope. R-32 and R-125 make up R-410A.

[e032] core
Which release of refrigerant is NOT a violation of the venting prohibition?
- Opening a charged system to the air to save recovery time
- Venting the vapor left in a system after liquid has been recovered
- Releasing refrigerant from a recovery cylinder to make room for more
* A small release while connecting or disconnecting hoses during charging, using low-loss fittings
> "De minimis" releases that happen during good-faith efforts to recover or recycle, such as hose connection losses, are not violations. Knowingly releasing any other refrigerant is illegal.

[e033] core
When is a refrigerant release considered "de minimis" under Section 608?
- Whenever less than one pound is released
- Whenever the refrigerant has zero ozone depletion potential
* When it happens during good-faith attempts to recapture, recycle or safely dispose of refrigerant
- When the owner of the equipment gives permission
> The Clean Air Act allows only de minimis releases tied to good-faith recovery, recycling or disposal, done with the required practices and certified equipment. No weight limit or owner permission makes venting legal.

[e034] core
A technician adds nitrogen to a fully charged R-22 system and then vents the mixture instead of recovering it. This is:
- Legal, because nitrogen with a trace of R-22 is a leak-test gas
* Illegal, because the refrigerant had to be recovered before nitrogen was added
- Legal, as long as the mixture is mostly nitrogen
- Legal for systems under 50 pounds
> A nitrogen/R-22 mixture used as a holding charge or leak-test gas is not a refrigerant release, but a technician may not dodge recovery by adding nitrogen to a charged system.

[e035] core

- R-134a
- R-410A
- R-123
> EPA exempts carbon dioxide, nitrogen and water in any application. HFCs such as R-134a and R-410A, and HCFCs such as R-123, may not be knowingly vented.

[e037] core

- A knowing-venting violation every time it happens

- Only regulated if the refrigerant is a CFC

- As a single fine per company per year

[e041] core
Which term means removing refrigerant from a system and storing it in an external container, without necessarily testing or processing it?
* Recover
- Recycle
- Reclaim
- Retrofit
> To recover is just to remove refrigerant and store it in an external container. Recycling cleans it and reclaiming reprocesses it to a purity standard.

[e042] core
To "recycle" refrigerant means to:
- Return it to the manufacturer for credit
* Clean it for reuse with oil separation and filter-driers that reduce moisture, acidity and particles
- Reprocess it to AHRI 700 purity and prove it with a laboratory analysis
- Destroy it in an approved incinerator
> Recycling cleans refrigerant, usually on the job site or at the shop, by separating oil and passing it through filter-driers. It is not tested to a purity standard, which is what reclaiming means.

[e043] core
To "reclaim" refrigerant means to:
- Recover it into a gray-and-yellow cylinder
- Filter it once through a suction-line filter-drier
* Reprocess it to the AHRI 700 purity specification and verify it by chemical analysis
- Return it to the same system after a repair
> Reclaimed refrigerant must meet AHRI Standard 700 purity, verified by laboratory analysis, and may only be reclaimed by an EPA-certified reclaimer.

[e044] core
Recovered refrigerant is going to be sold to a different equipment owner. Before the sale it must be:
- Recycled on site with a filter-drier
- Tested with an acid test kit by the technician
- Mixed with new refrigerant of the same type
* Reclaimed by an EPA-certified reclaimer
> Recovered refrigerant may go back into the same appliance, or another appliance owned by the same owner, without reclaiming. Before it changes ownership it must be reclaimed to AHRI 700 by a certified reclaimer.

[e045] core
Without having it reclaimed first, a technician may put recovered refrigerant back into:
* The same appliance, or another appliance belonging to the same owner
- Any customer's appliance, as long as it is the same refrigerant type
- Only appliances with a charge under 5 pounds
- A new appliance being sold to a different customer
> Recovered refrigerant may go back into the same appliance or another appliance with the same owner. Using it in another owner's equipment requires reclaiming first.

[e046] core
Why should different refrigerants never be mixed in the same recovery cylinder?
- Mixing makes the cylinder heavier than its rated tare weight
- Mixed refrigerants always explode
* The mixture may be impossible to reclaim and may have to be destroyed
- EPA allows only one recovery cylinder per technician
> Reclaimers cannot separate most mixed refrigerants, so a mixed cylinder usually has to be destroyed, at extra cost. Use a separate, labeled cylinder for each refrigerant.

[e049] core
In the ASHRAE 34 safety classification "A2L", what do the letter and the "2L" stand for?
- Higher toxicity; highly flammable
* Lower toxicity; lower flammability
- Lower toxicity; no flame propagation
- Higher toxicity; lower flammability
> "A" means lower toxicity and "2L" means lower flammability (a subclass of class 2 with a slow burning velocity). R-32, R-454B and R-1234yf are A2L.

[e050] core
What property separates a class 2L (lower flammability) refrigerant from other class 2 refrigerants?
- A boiling point below 0 °F
- A GWP below 150
* A maximum burning velocity of 10 cm/s or less
- An ozone depletion potential of zero
> ASHRAE 34 places class 2 refrigerants with a burning velocity of 10 cm/s or less in subclass 2L. They are hard to ignite and burn slowly, but they are still flammable.

[e053] core

[e054] core
Why is a large refrigerant leak in a basement or other enclosed space dangerous even if the refrigerant has low toxicity?
- Refrigerant vapor rises and collects at the ceiling, where it ignites
* Refrigerant vapor is heavier than air and can displace oxygen, causing suffocation
- Refrigerant vapor reacts with concrete to form acids
- Refrigerant always freezes the air in the room
> Most refrigerant vapors are heavier than air, collect in low areas and push out oxygen. Leave the area, ventilate it, and use self-contained breathing apparatus if you must go back in.

- Carbon monoxide only
- Ozone
* Phosgene
- Ammonia
> Burning chlorine-containing refrigerants (CFCs and HCFCs) can form phosgene, along with hydrochloric and hydrofluoric acids.

[e056] core

- Sulfur dioxide
* Hydrofluoric acid (hydrogen fluoride)
- Nothing; HFCs do not decompose

[e057] core
What is the correct way to pressurize a system with dry nitrogen for a leak test?
* Through a pressure regulator, with a pressure relief valve in the line
- Straight from the cylinder valve, opened slowly
- Through the recovery machine
- With the system compressor running
> A nitrogen cylinder can be over 2,000 psig. Always use a pressure regulator and a relief valve, and never go above the system's rated test pressure.

[e058] core
Why must oxygen or compressed air never be used to pressurize a refrigeration system?
- They are more expensive than nitrogen
* Oxygen or air mixed with oil under pressure can explode
- They have no smell, so leaks cannot be found
- They are heavier than refrigerant vapor
> Oxygen with compressor oil under pressure can ignite violently. Compressed air also brings in moisture. Use dry nitrogen.

[e059] core
Liquid refrigerant spraying on the skin or in the eyes mainly causes:
- Chemical burns from acids in new refrigerant
* Frostbite, because the liquid boils off and absorbs heat very quickly
- Electric shock
- Radiation burns
> Liquid refrigerant flashing to vapor can freeze tissue on contact. Wear safety glasses with side shields (or goggles) and gloves when handling refrigerant.

[e060] core

- AHRI 700

> ASHRAE 15 is the refrigeration safety standard (machinery rooms, detectors, relief discharge). ASHRAE 34 assigns refrigerant numbers and safety groups, AHRI 700 is the purity standard and AHRI 740 is the recovery-equipment test standard.

[e061] core
What is the safe way to warm a refrigerant cylinder to raise its pressure?
- With a propane torch, keeping the flame moving
- By placing it against a running compressor
* With warm water or a cylinder heater, never above about 125 °F (52 °C)
- By leaving it in a closed vehicle in the sun
> Never use an open flame or let a cylinder get hotter than about 125 °F. Heat raises pressure and liquid expansion, which can rupture the cylinder.

[e062] core
A refillable refrigerant recovery cylinder should be filled to no more than what share of its capacity?
- 60%
* 80%
- 90%
- 100%
> Fill recovery cylinders to no more than 80% of capacity by liquid volume (check by weight on a scale) to leave room for the liquid to expand as it warms.

[e063] core
Why is an overfilled refrigerant cylinder so dangerous?
- The relief valve cannot open on an overfilled cylinder
* As it warms, the expanding liquid can fill the cylinder and create enormous hydrostatic pressure
- Refrigerant vapor becomes flammable above 80% fill
- Overfilled cylinders cannot be shipped by DOT
> When a cylinder is liquid-full, a small temperature rise causes a huge pressure rise and can burst it. That is why recovery cylinders are filled to 80% at most.

[e064] core

- Every year
- Every 2 years
* Every 5 years
- Every 10 years
> The standard interval for refillable DOT recovery cylinders is a hydrostatic retest every five years, with the test date stamped on the cylinder. Do not fill a cylinder that is past its retest date.

[e065] core

> Disposable cylinders are single-trip containers. Refilling them is illegal and dangerous, and they must never be used for recovery.

[e066] core
